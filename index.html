About the data

Source. Monthly near-surface air temperature (tas, table Amon) from 9 CMIP6 models, ensemble member r1i1p1f1, read from the public CMIP6 Zarr bucket on Google Cloud: . Each model contributes its historical run (1950–2014) and four ScenarioMIP runs (2015–2100):

  • SSP1-2.6: strong mitigation, net-zero CO₂ around mid-century.
  • SSP2-4.5: middle of the road, roughly current policies.
  • SSP3-7.0: regional rivalry, emissions roughly double by 2100.
  • SSP5-8.5: fossil-fuelled development, the highest-emissions pathway.

Transformations.

  1. Annual means of the monthly values.
  2. City values: bilinear interpolation of each model's grid to the city-centre coordinates. Model grids are 1–2.8° wide (roughly 100–300 km), so a "city" value describes the surrounding region. It does not capture urban heat islands.
  3. Anomalies: every model's temperature minus that model's own 1995–2014 average at the same place. Models disagree about absolute temperature by several degrees, but they agree much better about change, so we pool the changes.
  4. Ensemble: median across the 9 models; the band shows the minimum and maximum model.
  5. Map and ranking: decade means (e.g. 2050–2059) of the annual anomaly. The map grid is re-interpolated to a common 2.5° grid. Time-series lines: 9-year centred running mean, so year-to-year weather noise doesn't hide the trend.
  6. The colour scale and ranking axis are fixed across pathways and decades, so a colour or position always means the same number of degrees.

The "warming hole" story refers to the cooler patch south of Greenland that most CMIP6 models simulate as the Atlantic overturning circulation weakens; the page shows the model output and does not test that mechanism.

Preprocessing code: prep/fetch.py and prep/build.py in this repository.

Design rationale

We wanted one page that answers two questions about a city you care about: how much hotter does it get, and how much of that depends on which emissions pathway the world ends up on. A global average is hard to relate to, and it hides how uneven the warming is. In the 2090s under SSP5-8.5, 48 of our 68 cities warm faster than the planet does. So the page starts from cities, and San Diego is selected by default because that's where we are.

Every view is tied to the same two controls, pathway and decade. Change either one and the map, the ranking, the sentence above the map and the line charts all update together. We did it this way so nobody has to remember what one chart said while they look at another.

The map uses the Equal Earth projection. The Arctic warms more than anywhere else, and Mercator would stretch it to several times its real area, so the darkest colours would also take up the most room for no good reason. Warming is shaded on one light-to-dark red scale because it is an amount with a zero, not two opposite directions. The scale is fixed at 0 to 9 °C for every pathway and every decade. We thought about rescaling it for each view so every map used the full range of colours, but then SSP1-2.6 in the 2030s would look as dark as SSP5-8.5 in the 2090s. With a fixed scale the mild pathway looks pale, which is the correct reading. The city dots are filled from the same scale, so a dot reads as a sample of the map around it.

A map is good at "where" and bad at "how much". It's hard to tell +4.4 from +3.8 by shade. That's what the ranking is for. Each city is a row, each pathway is a dot on one shared axis, and the grey bar from a city's lowest dot to its highest is the part of its warming that is still a choice. We considered grouped bars, but four bars for each of 68 cities is 272 bars and you can't read that. The sort buttons are there because each order shows something different. Sorted by latitude, the warming mostly climbs as you go north (Reykjavík is the exception, and the last story button is about it). Sorted by pathway gap, Yakutsk comes out on top: its lowest and highest pathways are 5.4 °C apart by the 2090s.

The four pathways go from low to high emissions, so their colours are ordered too: blue, gold, orange, dark red. We checked the palette with a colour-blindness tool to make sure neighbouring pathways can still be told apart with red-green colour blindness. Gold is the weak one. It doesn't contrast well with a white background. We kept it because the order mattered more to us, and we don't rely on colour alone anywhere: the pathway name is written out on the buttons and the ranking legend, and again in every tooltip.

For comparing cities over time we used small multiples, one panel per city, with the same y-axis in every panel. Four cities on one chart would have meant 16 lines. Inside a panel colour always means pathway, same as everywhere else on the page. The shaded band is the range across the nine models, and we only draw it for the selected pathway because four overlapping bands were unreadable. The dashed line is the global average under that pathway, so you can see whether the city is ahead of the planet or behind it.

The data choices matter for reading the charts too. Models disagree about absolute temperature by several degrees but much less about change, so each model is compared with its own 1995–2014 average before we combine them. That baseline is the last 20 years of the historical runs, so the future lines start where the baseline ends. We take the median across models and not the mean, because CanESM5 runs hot and would pull a mean up. The map and ranking use decade averages, so one step of the slider is one decade and a single odd year can't move a city up the ranking.

On interaction, the pathway buttons and the decade slider are the main thing. Drag the slider to the 2020s and press play: the four dots in each ranking row start almost on top of each other and drift apart decade by decade. In the 2030s the gap between the lowest and highest pathway is about 0.1 °C for the median city. By the 2090s it is 3.2 °C. A static chart can show where things end up, but not that the gap opens this late, and that was the point we most wanted to get across.

The region menu filters the ranking and fades the other cities on the map. The headline sentence is recomputed for that region as well. Zoom and pan are on the map because the European and East Asian cities sit on top of each other at world scale. Hovering anywhere on the map gives that grid cell's value, and hovering a city gives all four pathways with the model range. Clicking a city, on the map or in the ranking, adds it to the comparison. It holds up to four cities and the oldest one drops off when you add a fifth.

The four story buttons were added after the first version. Each one sets the controls to a finding we thought was worth seeing and explains it in a sentence or two, so a first-time reader has somewhere to start. Touch any control afterwards and you're out of the story and exploring on your own.

Two things we're less sure about. A "city" here is really a model grid cell 100 to 300 km across, so the numbers describe the region around the city and miss the urban heat island completely. We don't know how much extra that adds for somewhere like Delhi. And we used one run per model, so the band shows how far the nine models disagree with each other. It is not a full uncertainty range.

Development process

Aryen built the data pipeline and the page. Jayden and Haoyu tested it, gave feedback and helped with the checkpoint video. In total it came to roughly 20 people-hours.

Most of that time went on the data. CMIP6 on Google Cloud is stored as Zarr, and the catalogue alone is an 82 MB CSV. prep/fetch.py picks the nine models, pulls monthly temperature for the historical run and the four pathways, and caches it. Two models, MPI-ESM1-2-LR and NorESM2-LM, kept failing with an error about a "non-monotonic index". Their stores have overlapping chunks, so some timestamps were duplicated and out of order, and we had to drop the duplicates and sort by time before slicing by year. prep/build.py then writes two static JSON files, about 600 KB for the cities and 900 KB for the map grid, so the page needs no server.

On the page itself the map was the hard part. The grid is drawn on a canvas underneath the SVG, with the cells batched into 60 colour bins, so that it can redraw on every zoom step and on every decade of the animation without lagging. The land outline and the city dots stay in SVG on top so they can be hovered and clicked. Double-click resets the zoom, and that broke when the map wasn't zoomed at all: D3 tried to animate from the starting view to itself and produced NaN, so now it only animates if you have actually zoomed.
